# Curriculum-Aligned Invention, Engineering and Local Development Policy

Status: proposed product policy for review in PR #306. This policy document does not itself activate invention workflows in the live app.

## 1. Purpose

PacEdu should progressively help students learn to identify needs, invent useful equipment, adapt existing technologies, repair or improve products, and design systems that respond to real local problems. Learners should explore how locally available resources and knowledge from other countries can be combined responsibly to support community wellbeing, environmental sustainability, productivity, accessibility, resilience and national development.

Examples may include boats and safer marine transport, agricultural tools, water collection and filtration demonstrations, renewable-energy models, food storage, assistive devices, low-cost scientific equipment, computers and software, transport systems, and conceptual designs inspired by cars or aircraft. Examples are not claims that a particular curriculum prescribes every project.

## 2. Mandatory curriculum alignment

No formal invention worksheet, design challenge, practical activity, project, revision question, mock examination or examination item may be labelled curriculum-aligned until it is mapped to a verified achievement indicator, learning outcome, concept or skill for the applicable country/jurisdiction, curriculum version, class/year/form and subject.

Each learning item must record:
- country/jurisdiction, class/year/form, subject and curriculum version/effective date;
- authoritative syllabus/framework source, URL, document title/version/date and page/section;
- exact indicator/outcome/concept/skill identifier and wording where available;
- measurable student objective and prerequisites;
- the design or engineering process that teaches the outcome;
- assessment task, answer guidance/rubric and success criteria measuring the same outcome;
- ability-band adaptation, accessible alternatives, materials, cost, safety controls and required supervision;
- prototype/design version, evidence of testing, reviewer/date and change history.

Use authoritative current sources. Do not invent indicator IDs or present a broad topic as an exact official indicator. Do not transfer indicators across countries or curriculum versions. If the exact mapping cannot be verified, mark the item `outcome-mapping-pending`; it may be offered as a clearly labelled supplemental design exploration subject to teacher review, but not as formally curriculum-aligned or as a replacement for required curriculum content. Interdisciplinary projects must map each subject's contribution separately where applicable.

## 3. Learner invention and engineering cycle

1. **Identify a real need:** Observe an issue in school, home, community, industry or the environment. Define who experiences the problem and what evidence shows that it matters.
2. **Research existing solutions:** Review local knowledge and products, reliable technical sources, patents or open designs where appropriate, and technologies used in other countries. Record sources, date, limitations, licensing and cultural considerations.
3. **Set design requirements:** Describe what the device should do, who will use it, measurable success criteria, size/weight/capacity where relevant, affordability, accessibility, durability, repairability, safety and environmental impacts.
4. **Inventory available resources:** Consider locally available materials, tools, skills, workshops, repair services and maintenance capacity. Identify imported components or specialist support needed. Do not assume a resource is available without checking.
5. **Generate alternatives:** Draw, describe or model multiple possible designs. Explain the principles behind them and compare cost, feasibility, performance, safety, maintenance, environmental effects and who benefits or bears risks.
6. **Choose and justify a design:** Select an option using explicit criteria and evidence. Identify uncertainties and aspects needing expert review.
7. **Build an appropriate prototype:** Start with drawings, cardboard models, simulations, small-scale models, code or other low-risk prototypes. Use age-appropriate tools and approved adult supervision.
8. **Test safely:** Plan fair tests and collect relevant measurements. Never test an unapproved or unsafe prototype on people, animals, public infrastructure or occupied vehicles/vessels/aircraft.
9. **Improve iteratively:** Compare results with the criteria, record failures and limitations, seek feedback, revise the design and retest safely.
10. **Plan real-world use:** Estimate costs, sourcing, maintenance, spare parts, repair skills, disposal/recycling, permissions, standards and regulatory approvals. Distinguish a classroom prototype from a product ready for use or sale.
11. **Communicate and reflect:** Present drawings, materials, method, measurements, sources, results, limitations, safety review and next steps. Credit other people's designs and respect intellectual property.
12. **Consider local development:** Explain the likely usefulness, affordability, environmental effects, possible skills or jobs supported and any unintended consequences. Do not claim an invention has improved the national economy without appropriate evidence.

## 4. Local resources and international technology

Students may learn from technologies used in other countries and adapt ideas to local conditions. Adaptation should consider local climate, geography, culture, language, available materials, energy, repair capacity, supply chains, cost, accessibility, safety standards and environmental impacts. Imported designs should not simply be copied without checking whether they fit the local problem.

Local and Indigenous knowledge should be treated respectfully. Seek consent where required, credit knowledge holders, avoid exposing restricted or sacred knowledge, and do not assume community knowledge is universally applicable without testing the relevant claims safely.

The aim is not technological isolation or dependency: learners should understand when local production is feasible, when importing a component is sensible, and when collaboration with skilled professionals or international partners is necessary.

## 5. Example project families

These are illustrative project ideas. They require exact curriculum mapping before formal publication.

- **Boats and marine transport:** Draw and compare hull shapes; make a small non-occupant scale model; investigate buoyancy, stability, materials, corrosion, fuel/energy use and marine litter. Do not build or trial a student-built vessel for passengers without qualified engineering review, applicable approvals and safety certification.
- **Cars and land transport:** Model energy transfer, braking concepts, rolling resistance or safer pedestrian access. Use simulations or non-powered models. Do not modify or road-test real vehicles as a school activity without qualified professionals and legal approvals.
- **Aircraft and aviation:** Make paper gliders or safe scale models to investigate lift-related concepts, drag, balance and materials. Do not build, modify, fly or test crewed aircraft or dangerous powered devices as an unsupervised student project.
- **Agriculture and food:** Design low-risk seedling tools, compost systems, irrigation models, shade structures or food-waste measurement tools. Evaluate water use, cost, durability and safe handling.
- **Water and sanitation:** Prototype rainwater collection models, leak alerts or educational filtration demonstrations. Never claim that an unvalidated prototype makes drinking water safe; drinking-water treatment and testing must follow authoritative health guidance.
- **Energy and climate resilience:** Model solar energy, insulation, efficient lighting, flood-warning concepts or safe household energy-awareness tools. Mains electricity, batteries with hazardous chemistry, pressure systems and structural installations require qualified supervision.
- **Computers and digital technology:** Create offline learning tools, accessible interfaces, local-language resources, simple sensors or privacy-preserving data displays. Include cybersecurity, privacy, maintenance, power and connectivity constraints.
- **Health and assistive technology:** Design accessible grips, non-clinical reminder prototypes or communication aids. Diagnostic or treatment devices must not be represented as clinically safe or effective without appropriate validation and authorisation.
- **Repair, reuse and circular design:** Safely investigate repairability, spare parts, recycling and waste reduction. Exclude hazardous electrical equipment, batteries, chemicals and sharp or contaminated waste unless handled under appropriate professional safety procedures.

## 6. Age and ability progression

Use four adaptable learning bands:
- **Supported Start:** Notice a need, sketch or build a simple model from safe classroom materials, and explain what it is meant to do.
- **Developing:** Compare two designs, identify materials and describe a simple test with teacher support.
- **Working at Class Goal:** Set measurable criteria, produce a prototype, record test data and improve the design using evidence.
- **Extension:** Analyse trade-offs, costs and uncertainties; use technical research or simulation; compare iterations; justify design choices and propose a safe feasibility plan.

Adapt complexity to the verified curriculum, learner age, demonstrated skill and accessibility needs. ECE and younger learners should use drawing, sorting, simple construction and guided observation. Older learners can progress to measurements, coding, engineering calculations, technical documentation, budgeting, simulation and supervised vocational practice as specified by the applicable syllabus.

## 7. Safety, ethics and regulation

Require a risk assessment proportionate to the project, teacher/adult supervision, permission and specialist review where needed. Students must not handle hazardous materials or machinery without suitable training and controls; build or test load-bearing structures, public transport, boats carrying people, road-going vehicles, powered aircraft, electrical mains systems, pressure vessels or clinical devices without qualified oversight and applicable approvals. Do not conduct dangerous experiments or place the public at risk.

Products intended for sale or real-world deployment need suitable testing, quality control, product safety, regulatory compliance, truthful claims and consideration of liability. A classroom prototype is not automatically a safe product. Use age-appropriate intellectual-property and attribution practices; avoid copying protected designs or using another person's work without permission.

## 8. Evidence and assessment

Assess students on:
- verified curriculum learning and correct use of scientific/technical concepts;
- how clearly they define the problem and research existing solutions;
- quality of sketches, specifications and reasoning;
- suitability and responsible use of resources;
- fairness and repeatability of tests, data quality and interpretation;
- safety, accessibility, cost and environmental considerations;
- evidence-based improvements, reflection and communication;
- teamwork, attribution and honest reporting of limitations.

Do not grade a learner only on whether the prototype works perfectly or whether they can afford expensive materials. Provide low-cost and no-build alternatives, simulations and accessible options. The quality of reasoning, evidence and iteration matters more than expensive equipment.

## 9. Publication gates and tests

A formal curriculum-aligned project may be published only after:
- its authoritative curriculum source and exact outcome/concept are verified;
- the activity genuinely teaches that outcome and assessment measures it;
- class, subject, version and prerequisites match;
- ability/accessibility adaptations are present;
- material, cost and risk review is complete;
- any real-world deployment claim is supported by relevant evidence and expert review.

Automated checks should flag missing or unverifiable mappings, country/version/class/subject mismatch, missing assessment criteria, unsafe build/test instructions, unreviewed claims of product readiness, missing source attribution, and claims of economic or community impact unsupported by evidence.

Curriculum changes, new technology or a new product claim should trigger review, not automatic publication. Preserve version history and require educator or qualified technical review where appropriate.

A policy document is not proof that invention workflows, curriculum retrieval, prototype tracking, safety gates or automatic assessment are implemented in the live app. Those capabilities need separate implementation, tests, educator review and verified deployment.
